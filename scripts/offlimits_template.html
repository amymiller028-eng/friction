Ask a room full of employees which emotion feels most off-limits at work, and they’ll likely say anger.

In the American Emotions Survey of over 1,000 working Americans, anger was named the most off-limits workplace emotion by a wide margin. Of the who named an emotion, pointed to it, more than sadness, fear, and every other emotion combined.

The reasons were what you’d expect. People said anger is unprofessional, leads to things they regret, and damages the mood in the room.

I wasn’t surprised that anger topped the list. What made me curious was who chose it. When I broke the data down, I found that the people most convinced they can’t show anger are the people with the least power.

Anger and Leadership

Among employees who aren’t in leadership roles, said anger was off-limits. Among leaders, that dropped to . That’s a statistically significant gap, and it points to something most of us have felt: at work, anger is a privilege of rank.

Lower in the hierarchy, visible anger is a risk.* Without the skills to express it effectively, anger can be read as incompetence, insubordination, or instability, and it can become a reason to be passed over. In charge, the same emotion, and sometimes the same behavior, carries different weight. It can look like passion, conviction, or high standards.

Anger & Gender

Next I looked at gender. Most of the time I don’t find meaningful differences between men and women. This time I did. Women were significantly more likely than men to say anger was off-limits ( vs. ). And men were nearly twice as likely as women to say vulnerability or fear was the emotion they couldn’t show at work.

So, Who Gets to Be Angry at Work?

That’s the wrong question. Anger is an emotion and it carries information. It can tell us if something important is being blocked, ignored, or treated unfairly. In Friction: How Tension, Emotion, and Change Reveal Better Leaders, Maggie Sass & Ross Blankenship make the case that these tense moments are where leaders are revealed. Anger is one of the clearest signals that friction is present. When people feel they have to hide it, the signal gets lost and so does the chance to act on it.

Why This Matters for Anyone Who Leads

When a large share of your workforce believes an entire category of emotion is unsafe to express, you haven’t eliminated that emotion. Suppressed anger shows up as disengagement, gossip, attrition, and the resignation letter that seems to come out of nowhere. Anger expressed poorly does the same damage.

Friction organizes its practices into three levels, and they map well onto this data:

  1. Internal. Notice your own anger early and name what it’s telling you before it drives your behavior.
  2. Interpersonal. Express anger in a way that addresses the problem without damaging the relationship, and respond with curiosity when someone brings their frustration to you.
  3. Systemic. Look at the unwritten rules on your team. If only leaders and men can show frustration safely, your culture is filtering out valuable information from everyone else.

The leaders who handle this best don’t ban anger or indulge it. They’ve built emotional intelligence skills: reading emotion accurately, staying composed under pressure, and creating enough safety that people don’t have to hide what they feel until it’s too late to address.

The issue was never whether certain emotions belong at work. It’s whether your organization has taught people what to do with them.

* To read more about the history of anger at work and current views on it, see Roni Porat and Elizabeth Levy Paluck, “Anger at Work,” Frontiers in Social Psychology 2 (2024), Article 1337715, https://doi.org/10.3389/frsps.2024.1337715. Their studies find that workers who express anger are generally judged as less competent and less deserving of status, and that anger is seen as inappropriate except in response to clear wrongdoing.

Leading Through Friction

How we grouped the answers

The question was open-ended: “Which emotion feels most off-limits in your workplace, and why?” We sorted every answer into five core emotions: happy, sad, angry, afraid and ashamed. Each comes in high, medium and low intensity. Rage is high-intensity anger, frustration is medium and irritation is low. We coded only the emotion each person named, not words in their explanation. About the five core emotions

People describe the same feeling in many different words. One person says “rage,” another “frustration,” another “disdain,” and all three are pointing at anger. To compare answers fairly, we grouped them using the feeling-words table in Emotional Intelligence 2.0, TalentSmartEQ’s framework for building emotional vocabulary. It organizes hundreds of feeling words under five core emotions, each at three levels of intensity.

We used it for three reasons. It is the same framework TalentSmartEQ uses to teach people to recognize their emotions, so the results connect directly to how emotional intelligence is developed. It treats intensity as part of an emotion rather than a separate one, so irritation and rage count as the same underlying feeling. And it gives every answer a clear, repeatable home, so anyone can check how an answer was coded.
