The American Emotions Survey

We asked 1,188 working adults across the U.S. how emotion really shows up at work: what they feel, what they hide, what it costs them and what they need from their leaders. The results helped shape Friction: How Tension, Emotion, and Change Reveal Better Leaders, and Chapter 4 is built on them.

About the survey

TalentSmartEQ ran the American Emotions Survey to understand emotion at work from the inside: not how people score on an assessment, but how they experience their feelings on the job, which feelings their workplace allows and what holding them back costs.

The findings informed Friction by Ross Blankenship, PhD and Maggie Sass, PhD, and Chapter 4, “Leading Through Friction,” distills them into nine insights. Many results didn’t make it into the book, so we’re sharing them here too.

Leaders and non-leaders answered different sections. People with a formal leadership role rated their own behavior; everyone else rated their leader. That lets us compare how leaders see themselves with how employees see leaders.

What we asked about

  • Twelve emotions, felt at work and outside it
  • Which emotions feel off-limits at work, and why
  • Confidence managing strong positive and negative emotions
  • Emotional drain and feeling inauthentic
  • Workplace norms about showing emotion
  • Leadership behaviors, rated by leaders and by employees
  • Emotional intelligence, AI and the future of work

Chapter 4 Findings, In Detail

We already know where we’re going.In the book: Roughly 60% of respondents said emotional intelligence will matter even more in the future of work.

Survey question: “How important will emotional intelligence (EQ) be for human success at work as AI takes on more tasks?” (Top-2 = Very + Extremely important)

Response breakdown: 26.3% of respondents (313 people) said EQ will be Extremely important, and 33.1% said Important, an additional 23.8% said Moderately important. Millennials and Gen Z lead the way at 62–63%; Boomers and the Silent Generation are lowest, but still roughly half.

The “Emotion Is Weakness” Myth Is Officially DyingIn the book: 35% of working adults still agree that emotions are a weakness at work, and counterintuitively, Millennials hold that belief most strongly, not older generations.

Survey question: “To what extent do you agree — Showing emotions at work is seen as a sign of weakness” (Top-2 = Somewhat + Strongly agree)

Millennials, the generation most associated with popularizing emotional-intelligence language and “bringing your whole self to work”, endorse the emotions-as-weakness view at a higher rate than any other cohort, including Gen X and the Boomers who are often assumed to be the norm’s originators.

Leaders Write the Emotional Rule BookIn the book: More than half of respondents (53.6%) agree that when leaders hide their emotions, it creates more problems than it solves. Leaders themselves feel the most pressure to suppress: 66% of leaders vs 47% of non-leaders agree leaders feel more pressure to hide emotions than other employees.

Survey question: “To what extent do you agree — When leaders hide their emotions, it creates more problems than it solves” (Top-2 = Somewhat + Strongly agree)

In regression models controlling for age, gender, leader role and level, industry, and other emotional norms (see Model 1A, Appendix), agreement with this item is a moderate, statistically significant predictor of both emotional drain (standardized β = .135, p < .001) and inauthenticity (β = .186, p < .001).

Leaders vs. Non-Leaders: Emotional Drain

Leaders report meaningfully more emotional drain than the people they lead, not less. This is consistent with the chapter’s “leader-as-thermostat” idea: Leaders don’t just feel pressure to hide emotion, they also personally absorb more of the organization’s emotional weight.

Enlightened but Exhausted: The Cost of ProgressIn the book: Emotional drain rises steadily by generation, and younger workers hold the most emotionally open values while paying the highest emotional cost for the gap between those values and the workplaces they’ve inherited.

The generational gap is nearly a full point (1.29) between the oldest and youngest cohorts, though the gap between Millennials and Gen Z specifically is small (0.12).

Supporting comparison — emotional-openness measures where younger cohorts diverge from older ones

External context (not from this survey): A 2024 UKG survey found 83% of Gen Z frontline workers report feeling burned out (higher than any other working group). Eagle Hill Consulting found 66% of Gen Z workers report burnout, vs. 37% of Boomers, and that Gen Z and Millennial workers reach peak burnout around age 25 — roughly 17 years earlier than the survey’s national average of 42.

Younger Workers Are the Most Emotionally OpenIn the book: Younger cohorts are more likely to see their workplace as accepting of emotional expression, more likely to say leaders hiding emotions creates problems, and more likely to feel comfortable when colleagues express emotion, with Millennials and Gen Z together leading a cultural shift..

Top-2 Box across three emotional-openness measures, by generation

Millennials and Gen Z are essentially tied for the lead across all three measures. Millennials edge out Gen Z on two of the three, and the two cohorts trade the top spot on the third by about a point. Both groups run well ahead of Gen X and the Boomers.

Across three systemic-norm indicators combined into a single index, the average American workplace scores 3.04 out of 5 — squarely in the middle, meaning most workplaces send mixed rather than clearly open or clearly restrictive emotional signals when colleagues express emotion, with Millennials and Gen Z together leading a cultural shift.

EQ Is Your Personal Battery SaverIn the book: One EQ skill in particular, confidence in managing negative emotions, is significantly associated with lower emotional drain. Average EQ scores in the sample sit around a healthy 3.7 out of 5 (rescaled from a 1–6 scale).

What actually predicts lower emotional drain (Model 1A, full controls)

The specific, learnable skill of “confidence managing negative emotions” is what buffers drain — not overall EQ as a single composite score.

You Can’t “Out-EQ” a Broken SystemIn the book: Even after controlling for individual EQ, the workplace norm that “emotions are a weakness” remains a powerful, independent predictor of drain and inauthenticity; culture matters independent of personal skill.

“Emotions are a weakness at work” as a predictor, controlling for EQ (Model 1A)

This is the single strongest and most robust regression result in the entire dataset. Systemic emotional norms average 3.04 out of 5 across the sample, meaning most workplaces are mixed-to-restrictive rather than clearly emotionally safe.

EQ Sees More, Feels MoreIn the book: In workplaces where emotion is treated as weakness, higher EQ steepens the curve. Emotionally aware people notice the punitive culture more acutely, and that awareness itself carries a cost.

EQ × Weakness-Norm interaction (moderation model)

The two other tested interactions — EQ × leader-pressure-to-hide, and confidence-managing-negative-emotions × leader-pressure-to-hide — were not statistically significant (p = .302 and p = .432, respectively). The one reliable moderation effect in the data is specifically EQ awareness sharpening the felt cost of a weakness-punishing culture.
